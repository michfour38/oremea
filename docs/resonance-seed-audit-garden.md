# Resonance seed audit — The Garden

Internal working record for later cross-room comparison. This file does not drive participant-facing content.

## Room boundary

- Room: The Garden
- Theme: Care, capacity, and nourishment
- Seed job: collect what actually gives, costs, carries, restores, circulates, gets requested, and can be sustained; do not tell the participant what care means about them
- Voice: plain, human, concrete, recognisable
- Movement: receive → give → notice limits early → see how care moves → ask clearly → make arrangements workable → choose what changes
- Evidence preference: specific help received, observable after-effects, giving patterns, early running-low cues, distribution of labour/resources, direct requests, concrete arrangements, participant-chosen changes
- Answer tolerance: a useful seed must still work with short answers, uncertainty, `nothing`, `nobody`, `both`, humour, contradiction, correction, or rejection of the premise
- Interpretation boundary: do not turn over-giving, difficulty receiving, fatigue, imbalance, or support patterns into proof of worth, trauma, attachment, resentment, love, selfishness, martyrdom, or hidden motive
- First-read clarity boundary: the literal question must make sense on the first read; contrast may invite the answer but must never require mental reversal, abstract translation, or rereading to discover what is being asked

## Boundary with The Hearth and The Mirror

- **The Hearth** asks where and with whom participation becomes easier or harder.
- **The Mirror** asks what the participant repeatedly does inside interaction and what tends to happen next.
- **The Garden** asks what care and support actually do in lived life: what restores capacity, what consumes it, who carries what, what gets requested, and what can keep working over time.

Overlap is intentional only when a different evidence target is being collected. A question about helping belongs in The Garden when the target is capacity, resources, labour, receiving, asking, circulation, or sustainability—not belonging or recurring interaction roles by themselves.

## Cross-room comparison notes after three rooms

- **Hearth / Garden overlap:** both can ask about what other people do. Hearth uses those actions as evidence of welcome, fit, and room to participate; Garden uses them as evidence of practical/emotional care, capacity gain, labour, support, and resource movement.
- **Mirror / Garden overlap:** both can observe repeated helping or carrying. Mirror asks what recurring role the participant takes and what interaction follows. Garden asks what that helping costs, provides, distributes, or requires to remain workable.
- **Garden centre of gravity:** care as an actual flow of time, labour, attention, resources, rest, help, and responsibility.
- **Distinct Garden evidence forms introduced:** absent-care visibility, capacity before/after help, giving-cost maps, early running-low cues, care-infrastructure maps, full-handover evidence, direct requests, need/preference separation, operational care design.
- **Watch in later rooms:** values, attraction, triggers, conflict, and future-design rooms should not reuse capacity/care language as a shortcut for their own constructs. If a later question is really asking who carries, gives, receives, pays, rests, asks, or replenishes, it probably belongs here.

## Cross-room comparison fields to retain

Compare every seed on at least these axes:

1. **Construct overlap** — is another room collecting the same thing under different language?
2. **Evidence form** — cue, behaviour, episode, preference, distinction, choice, sequence, outcome, participant-stated meaning.
3. **Perspective** — self, other person, group/space, mutual interaction, system/resource flow.
4. **Time orientation** — present/general, recent episode, ordinary week, current decision, future participation.
5. **Contrast structure** — whether the question offers a useful foothold without deciding the answer.
6. **Interpretation load** — how much meaning the participant must manufacture before they can answer.
7. **Premise risk** — whether the wording assumes an emotion, motive, identity, injury, virtue, deficit, imbalance, or causal story.
8. **Answer-range tolerance** — whether `IDK`, `nothing`, `nobody`, `both`, a correction, or a short concrete answer remains valid data.
9. **Mirror yield** — whether the answer is likely to give the Daily Mirror observable material rather than polished self-theory.
10. **Participation yield** — whether the question gives the participant something they can notice, ask, receive, give, stop, hand over, structure, or choose without prescribing the answer.
11. **First-read clarity** — whether the literal question is immediately understandable without mentally reversing absence, unpacking abstract nouns, or rereading the sentence.
